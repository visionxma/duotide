Anthropic's IPO: What's Confirmed and What's Still a Rumor

Quick answer

As of September 2026, what's confirmed is that Anthropic filed a confidential S-1 with the SEC on June 1 and that its latest round, Series H, valued it at $965 billion. There's no date, ticker, or share price yet. The roughly $2 trillion valuation circulating in the market is a bank projection, not an official figure.

Whenever a major company files a confidential IPO application, the market fills the information gap with speculation. That's what happened with Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI assistant, after it submitted a confidential S-1 to the SEC, the United States securities regulator.

This piece separates what has been announced from what remains behind-the-scenes talk. The numbers below reflect the information disclosed as of September 2026, according to the reporting this piece is based on, and may have changed by the time you're reading it.

What a confidential S-1 is

The S-1 is the registration form a company files with the SEC before selling shares to the public for the first time. Since 2017, any company can submit the first version confidentially: the regulator reviews the document and asks questions without the market seeing the content. The public version must be disclosed at least 15 days before the roadshow, the round of presentations to investors. In practice, this means that while the filing is confidential, no one outside the company knows revenue, profit, share count, or the intended price.

What's confirmed

  • On June 1, 2026, Anthropic filed a confidential S-1 with the SEC, the first formal step toward a public offering, with no set date.
  • The last funding round with a disclosed value was Series H, worth $65 billion, closed on May 28, 2026, which valued the company at $965 billion.
  • There's still no ticker, share price, or defined number of shares.
  • The most-discussed listing window is the fourth quarter of 2026, but the company hasn't confirmed that date.

What's still a rumor

The figure circulating the most is a valuation of around $2 trillion at the eventual listing. That number doesn't come from an official pricing document: it stems from revenue projections between $190 billion and $200 billion through 2028, discussed by banks that typically advise this kind of deal. It's a presentation reference, not something written in a prospectus.

A confidential filing can sit under review for months before any detail becomes public. Anyone setting an entry-price expectation today is betting on a valuation that no outsider can verify.

Practical example: what the rumored numbers imply

Running the numbers helps show the size of the bet baked into the rumor. All the figures below come from the numbers cited above; the calculation is illustrative and not a price estimate.

  • Jump from the last round: $2 trillion ÷ $965 billion = 2.07. The valuation would more than double in a few months compared with the price paid by Series H investors.
  • Multiple on projected revenue: $2 trillion ÷ $200 billion = 10 times the revenue estimated for 2028; with the $190 billion projection, 10.5 times. And that's on revenue that still needs to be reached.
  • Sensitivity to the multiple: if the market accepted paying 6 times instead of 10, the same $200 billion revenue would produce $1.2 trillion, 40% below the rumored figure. If revenue came in at $150 billion, at 10 times, that would be $1.5 trillion.

The point isn't to say which multiple is correct, but to show that small changes in assumptions move hundreds of billions of dollars. That's why the final price of an IPO is usually decided only in the last few days, after the roadshow.

A recent precedent: SpaceX

SpaceX's IPO, priced on June 12 at around $135 a share, shows a mechanic that tends to repeat. Before the listing, shares of companies linked to the space theme, such as Rocket Lab, Intuitive Machines, and Firefly Aerospace, nearly doubled in value just for being associated with the topic, since SpaceX itself wasn't yet tradable. On the day of the debut, Rocket Lab fell nearly 11% in the same session, with money rotating into the main asset.

Anthropic and SpaceX are companies in different sectors, so the comparison has its limits. But the pattern of expectation flowing into theme-adjacent assets before the event, and flowing out afterward, is common in highly anticipated offerings.

Who's affected by the theme besides Anthropic

According to the original reporting, AI computing infrastructure companies have been trading with this expectation on the radar, even without revenue coming directly from Anthropic: bitcoin miners that have started hosting AI computing capacity, data center companies, and cloud infrastructure companies with recent multibillion-dollar contracts.

Money around the theme shows up elsewhere too. Alphabet raised $85 billion in a single week in June 2026 to fund AI data centers. OpenAI filed its own confidential application a week after Anthropic, with a most recent valuation of $852 billion and an estimated listing in the second half of 2026, which could slip to 2027. Analysts cited in the reporting note that if the two offerings end up overlapping on the calendar, whichever one lists last could face more competition for the same pool of investors.

How to follow Anthropic's IPO

Official sources first

The concrete sign that the offering is near is the publication of the S-1 in the SEC's public database. That's where revenue, loss or profit, share structure, and risk factors show up. Until then, any share price is speculation.

Indicative range and final price

Close to the roadshow, the company discloses an indicative price range. The final price is set the night before the debut and can land above, within, or below that range, depending on demand.

Limitations of this analysis

Nothing here is a buy or sell recommendation. The numbers are the ones disclosed as of September 2026, and several dates cited are market estimates, not company commitments.

Common mistakes when following a highly anticipated IPO

  • Treating the $2 trillion valuation as a set price, when it's a behind-the-scenes projection.
  • Confusing a private round's valuation with the market value on the exchange.
  • Buying shares of theme-adjacent companies thinking they'll track the debut, when recent history, like Rocket Lab's, shows the opposite on the day of the event.
  • Believing listing dates that the company itself hasn't confirmed.
  • Ignoring that the debut price can land below the indicative range if demand disappoints.
