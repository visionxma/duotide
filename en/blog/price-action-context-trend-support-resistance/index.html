Price Action: Why a Candle Only Makes Sense in Context

Duotide Team · 09/24/2026 · 4 min read

It's common for a beginner to memorize the shape of a few candles and go hunting for that pattern on the chart, entering every time it appears. The problem is that the same candle, on the same asset, can mean completely different things depending on where it forms. Price action isn't about recognizing isolated shapes — it's about reading price behavior within a context.

This article is about that context: how trend, support and resistance levels, and the strength of the prior move turn an ordinary candle into a relevant signal, or completely dismantle a signal that looked good.

A single candle says almost nothing

A candle with a long lower wick can indicate rejection of lower prices and buying strength — but only if this happens in a spot on the chart where that rejection makes sense. The same candle forming in the middle of a price range, with no nearby level, tends to be just noise. Analyzing price action starts with reducing the importance of the isolated candle and increasing the importance of where it appears.

First step: identify the trend before looking at candles

Before looking for any entry signal, define the bigger picture: is the asset in an uptrend, a downtrend, or trading sideways between two prices? A simple way to check this is to look at the sequence of highs and lows over recent periods. Rising highs and lows indicate an uptrend; falling highs and lows indicate a downtrend; when this sequence isn't clear, the market is probably sideways.

A reversal signal against a strong trend has a different probability than a continuation signal in its favor. This doesn't mean avoiding trades against the trend, but requiring stronger confirmation when that happens.

Support and resistance give meaning to the signal

The same reversal candle gains weight when it appears in a region that has already worked as support or resistance before — a price zone where the market has already reacted more than once. This shows that other participants also see that level as relevant, which increases the chance of a similar reaction happening again.

The same candle forming midway, far from any previous level, carries much less information, even if its shape is identical to that of a classic signal.

Volume and distance from the prior move

Two additional details help filter out weak signals. The first is volume: a reversal candle accompanied by above-average volume tends to reflect a real shift in strength between buyers and sellers. The second is how far the prior move has already traveled — an exhaustion signal after a long decline makes more sense than the same signal right at the start of a move, when there is still more room to continue in the same direction.

A practical decision example

Suppose EUR/USD has been in a downtrend over recent weeks and reaches a support region at 1.0850, tested twice before. There, a reversal candle forms with a close near the period's high. With the underlying trend, a relevant level, and a reversal signal all aligned, the scenario is more consistent than if the same candle appeared at 1.0920, with no nearby support.

One possible plan would be to enter at 1.0855, with a stop at 1.0820 (35 points of risk) and a target at 1.0955, near the next resistance (100 points of return) — a risk-reward ratio of almost 1 to 3, calculated before entry, not after watching the price move in your favor.

Mistakes made by those who read candles out of context

  • Entering as soon as a familiar pattern appears, without checking the chart's trend.
  • Ignoring whether the candle is near a relevant support or resistance level.
  • Treating every signal against the trend as a guaranteed reversal.
  • Not considering volume or how far the move has already traveled.

No combination of trend, support, and candle eliminates the risk of loss — the market remains uncertain by nature. What changes is the quality of the information used to decide. Practice observing the context before the candle, on a platform like Duotide or on historical charts, and only then evaluate the shape of the isolated candle.
