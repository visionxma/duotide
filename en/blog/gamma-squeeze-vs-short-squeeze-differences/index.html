Gamma Squeeze vs Short Squeeze: What's the Real Difference

Short squeeze and gamma squeeze are two terms that tend to show up together whenever a stock suddenly spikes, but they describe different mechanisms, with different buyers behind the buying pressure. Confusing the two can lead to betting on a continuation of the move that doesn't actually have the same fuel behind it.

Short squeeze: those who sold borrowed shares being forced to buy back

A short squeeze starts with traders who sold borrowed shares, betting on a price decline. If the price rises instead of falling, those positions start accumulating losses. Margin calls, rising costs to keep borrowing the shares, or internal risk limits can force these sellers to buy back the shares to close the position. If many sellers try to buy back at the same time, that extra demand can push the price even higher, pressuring other sellers to do the same.

Gamma squeeze: those who sold call options adjusting their hedge

A gamma squeeze has a different origin, tied to the options market. When many investors buy call options on a stock, the seller of those options, usually a market maker, can end up with exposure that needs to be hedged. If that seller has a net negative gamma position, they may need to buy shares of the asset to hedge as the price rises. Since the rising price changes that exposure again, even more shares may need to be bought, creating a cycle that reinforces the rally itself.

The two can happen at the same time

A regular rally or relevant news can attract buyers of both stock and call options at the same time. If part of that options buying leaves market makers with a short gamma position, their hedging can add extra demand. The higher price can then pressure short sellers into buying back their positions. The two forces can feed off each other within the same move, which makes it hard to separate how much of the rally came from each one.

What public data can and can't prove

  • Call option volume: shows how many contracts were traded, but doesn't prove market makers ended up short gamma, because part of those options may have been sold by other investors or traded within structures with offsetting positions.
  • Open interest: shows how many contracts remain active, but doesn't reveal who's on each side of the trade.
  • Short interest: is usually released only twice a month, so a high number doesn't prove sellers are buying back right now, only that the position existed on the last reference date.

Price and volume rising together are consistent with either story, or with neither one, since regular buyers could also be behind the move.

The GameStop case in 2021: why a single label wasn't enough

The SEC's analysis of the GameStop case, in January 2021, found evidence that known short sellers bought shares during specific periods of the rally, suggesting part of the move came from buying back short positions. But that same analysis found this buyback represented only a small fraction of total buying volume, and that the price stayed elevated for weeks after the direct effect of the buyback should have faded. The analysis also noted heightened options activity, but much of it came from buying put options, and market makers were buying, not selling, call options, which doesn't match the expected pattern of a classic gamma squeeze. The conclusion was that sustained positive sentiment, more than either mechanism on its own, helped keep the price elevated for so long.

The risk of trying to jump in after the chart has already spiked

Chasing a squeeze after the move has already become visible usually means buying at the worst possible moment. Spreads can widen, options can get expensive because of elevated implied volatility, and a market order can be executed far from the last price shown on screen. Neither mechanism guarantees a next move, and a sharp rally can reverse as soon as the buyback or the options hedging loses steam. Defining the risk before entering remains more important than trying to guess which label best explains a move that's already happened.
