Gamma Squeeze vs Short Squeeze: What's the Real Difference

Quick answer

In a short squeeze, the forced buyers are short sellers, who buy back shares to limit losses when the price rises. In a gamma squeeze, the buyers are call sellers, usually market makers, who buy shares to hedge their position. The two can happen together, and public data rarely proves which one dominated.

Short squeeze and gamma squeeze tend to show up together whenever a stock suddenly spikes, but they describe different mechanisms, with different buyers behind the rise. Confusing the two leads to betting on the continuation of a move that may not have the fuel people imagine.

This guide explains who is forced to buy in each case, shows a numeric example of each mechanism, discusses what public data can and can't prove, and revisits the 2021 GameStop case.

What a short squeeze is

A short squeeze starts with investors who sold borrowed shares, betting on a decline. If the price rises, those positions rack up losses. Margin calls, more expensive stock borrowing fees, or internal risk limits can force these sellers to buy back the shares to close the position. If many do this at once, the extra demand pushes the price up and pressures other sellers to do the same.

Numeric example (hypothetical)

  • A stock has 2 million shares sold short and average volume of 1 million a day: that's 2 days of volume just to close out those positions (the metric called days to cover).
  • A seller who shorted 100,000 shares at $50.00 sees the price go to $60.00: a loss of 100,000 × 10.00 = $1,000,000.00.
  • If they buy back, that's another 100,000 shares of demand entering the market, right when the price is already rising.

What a gamma squeeze is

A gamma squeeze starts in the options market. When many investors buy call options, whoever sells them, usually a market maker, becomes exposed to a rise. To hedge, they buy shares of the underlying asset in proportion to the delta of the calls sold. Gamma measures how much that delta changes as the price moves. If the market maker is short gamma, every rise increases the delta and forces them to buy more shares, which can reinforce the rise itself.

Numeric example (hypothetical)

A market maker sold 10,000 call contracts, each on 100 shares, meaning exposure to 1,000,000 shares:

  • Stock at $50.00, delta 0.30: hedge = 0.30 × 1,000,000 = 300,000 shares bought.
  • Stock at $55.00, delta 0.55: hedge needed = 550,000 → buys another 250,000 shares.
  • Stock at $60.00, delta 0.80: hedge needed = 800,000 → buys another 250,000 shares.

Each step up triggered new buying, without any investor having changed their mind about the company. The effect is stronger when the options are near the strike price and expiration, where gamma is highest. The reverse also holds: if the price falls, the market maker sells shares and can accelerate the drop.

Real example: what public data shows and doesn't show

In practice, you don't see market makers' or short sellers' positions in real time. What exists are clues:

  • Call volume: shows how many contracts traded, but doesn't prove market makers ended up short gamma; some of those calls may have been sold by other investors or be part of structures with offsetting positions.
  • Open interest: shows how many contracts remain active, not who is on which side.
  • Short interest: in the United States it's published twice a month; a high number proves the position existed on the reference date, not that short sellers are buying back now.
  • Price and volume rising together: compatible with both stories, with neither, or with ordinary buyers.

The GameStop case in 2021

The SEC staff's report on GameStop, published in 2021, analyzed that January's rally. It found evidence that short sellers bought shares at specific points during the rise, which indicates covering of short positions. But that covering was a small fraction of the total buy volume, and the price stayed elevated after its direct effect should have faded.

The report also recorded elevated options activity, but much of it came from put buying, and market makers were buying, not selling, calls on a net basis, which doesn't match the pattern of a classic gamma squeeze. The conclusion was that sustained positive sentiment from buyers, more than either mechanism on its own, kept the price elevated for so long.

How to use this difference

Identify who is forced to buy

In a short squeeze, the forced buying comes from those short the stock. In a gamma squeeze, from those short calls who are hedging. Asking who is forced to buy, and how much is left to buy, helps separate narrative from mechanism.

The two can add up

A piece of news can attract stock buyers and call buyers at the same time. The market makers' hedging adds demand, the higher price pressures short sellers, and the two forces feed each other. That's why, even after the fact, it's hard to separate how much of the rise came from each one.

Limitations

Both mechanisms run out: once the shorts have covered or the options expire, the forced buying ends and the rally can reverse fast. No public indicator precisely shows how much fuel is left.

Common mistakes about squeezes

  • Calling every sharp rally a short squeeze without looking at short interest data.
  • Treating high call volume as proof of a gamma squeeze.
  • Entering after the chart has already spiked, when spreads widen, options get expensive from high implied volatility, and market orders fill far from the last price.
  • Forgetting the mechanism works both ways and can accelerate a decline.
  • Trading without a defined stop because the narrative seems strong.

To understand these mechanisms without risking money, follow a stock with a strong move on a demo account, note volume, news, and what the price did in the following days, and record whether your initial read held up. Defining the risk before entering still matters more than getting the label right on a move that's already happened.
