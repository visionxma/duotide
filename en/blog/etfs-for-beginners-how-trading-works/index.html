ETFs for Beginners: How Trading Actually Works

ETF stands for exchange traded fund. In practice, it's a basket of assets, like the stocks of an entire index, that can be bought and sold the same way as any stock, with a single trading ticker.

For anyone who has never traded an ETF, the process is simpler than it looks, but a few details make the difference between a smooth first experience and one full of doubts.

What you're actually buying

By buying an ETF share, you're buying a small fraction of a portfolio that replicates some specific index or strategy. An ETF that replicates a broad stock index, for example, gives you exposure to dozens or hundreds of companies in a single purchase, instead of requiring you to buy each stock individually.

This automatically delivers a basic level of diversification: the performance of a single company in the portfolio has limited impact on the ETF's total result, unlike what would happen if you had bought only that stock on its own.

How trading actually works

Unlike traditional funds, priced once a day, an ETF trades just like a stock, throughout the trading session, with the price changing on every closed trade. That lets you use the same order types common in the stock market:

  • Market order: buys or sells at the best price available at that moment.
  • Limit order: sets a maximum buy price or minimum sell price, only executing if the market reaches that level.
  • Stop order: automatically triggers a sell (or buy) if the price reaches a pre-defined level, useful for limiting losses.

What to check before your first trade

Before buying an ETF for the first time, a few points deserve attention:

  • What the ETF replicates: understand exactly which index, sector, or strategy is behind that fund, not just its commercial name.
  • Expense ratio: even a small one reduces returns over time. An ETF with a 0.10% annual fee costs R$ 10.00 a year for every R$ 10,000 invested, just in management fees.
  • Average trading volume: ETFs with low volume tend to have wider spreads, meaning a bigger difference between the buy and sell price, which makes trading more expensive.
  • Tracking error: check whether the ETF's historical performance actually closely follows the index it promises to replicate.

Practical example of a trade

Imagine an ETF trading at R$ 25.00 per share, with a R$ 0.02 spread between buy and sell. Buying 400 shares means a total investment of R$ 10,000.00. If the price rises to R$ 26.00, the position's value becomes R$ 10,400.00, a gross gain of R$ 400.00, before deducting brokerage costs and the expense ratio proportional to the period the position was held.

Common mistakes among beginners

A few slip-ups show up often among beginners:

  • Buying an ETF just for its name, without checking the actual composition of the portfolio behind it.
  • Ignoring the spread on low-volume ETFs, which eats into part of the result on short-term trades.
  • Concentrating all capital in a single ETF, even if it's already internally diversified, without considering other asset types in the overall portfolio.

Recommended first steps

For beginners, a prudent approach is usually: choose an ETF that replicates a broad, well-known index, confirm the expense ratio and traded volume, and trade a position size compatible with how much you're willing to risk, whether by using a demo account first or starting with smaller amounts on a platform like Duotide.

Investing in ETFs, despite the built-in diversification, still involves risk of capital loss, since the share price tracks the performance of the portfolio's assets. Diversification reduces some specific risks, but doesn't eliminate market risk as a whole.

Finally, it's worth periodically checking whether the chosen ETF still makes sense for your original goal. Changes to the replicated index's composition, an increase in the expense ratio, or a significant drop in traded volume are signs it's worth reviewing the position, instead of simply keeping it out of habit.
