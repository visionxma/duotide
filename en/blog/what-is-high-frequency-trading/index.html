What Is High-Frequency Trading (HFT) and How It Works

Quick answer

High-frequency trading, or HFT, is trading done by automated systems that send and cancel a huge volume of orders in milliseconds or microseconds. The gain per trade is tiny, but it becomes meaningful multiplied across millions of trades. The edge comes from speed and infrastructure, something out of reach for anyone trading manually.

When a stock's price changes by a fraction of a cent several times within the same second, there's almost never a person behind each change. Much of that movement comes from programs buying, selling, and canceling orders at a speed no human can follow.

Understanding how these systems work isn't about imitating them, but about knowing where the ordinary trader can compete and where they can't. In this guide we explain what HFT is, what it's after, how it affects liquidity, and what that changes about reading a chart.

What High-Frequency Trading Is

HFT is a subset of algorithmic trading. All HFT is done by algorithm, but not every algorithm is high-frequency. Three traits define HFT:

  • Speed: decisions and orders in milliseconds (thousandths of a second) or microseconds (millionths).
  • Volume: millions of orders a day, many of them canceled before they execute.
  • Extremely short positions: trades last from fractions of a second to a few minutes, and the system usually ends the day flat.

Why It Requires Its Own Infrastructure

Light in a fiber-optic cable travels about 200 kilometers per millisecond. For anyone fighting over microseconds, the physical distance to the exchange's server becomes a cost. That's why HFT firms rent space in the same data center as the exchange, a practice called colocation, use shorter communication routes, and run hardware optimized to shave off every bit of delay. This setup costs millions and is maintained by dedicated teams. A home internet connection, however fast, measures latency in tens of milliseconds: thousands of times slower.

What These Systems Are After

Market Making

The system keeps both buy and sell orders live at the same time and earns the spread between them, at very high volume. It's the most common strategy.

Arbitrage

The same asset, or assets linked to each other, like an ETF and the stocks that make it up, can briefly trade at slightly different prices in different venues. Whoever gets there first buys the cheaper one and sells the pricier one.

Very Short-Term Statistical Patterns

Some systems read the order flow and react to patterns that last seconds, like an imbalance between buy and sell orders in the book or the sequence of trades right after a news release.

All three strategies share one thing: they depend on getting there before the competition. When two systems spot the same opportunity, whoever executes first gets the gain, and the second one, usually, gets nothing. That's why the industry invests so much to shave off microseconds.

How to Calculate the Gain From an HFT Strategy

An illustrative example, with round numbers, shows why the gain per trade can be so small:

  • A net gain of $0.001 per share, a tenth of a cent, after costs.
  • 10 million shares traded per day: 0.001 × 10,000,000 = $10,000 per day.
  • Over 250 sessions: 10,000 × 250 = $2.5 million a year, before infrastructure costs.

For scale, we used real data: SPY, the ETF that tracks the S&P 500, averaged 49.6 million shares traded per day between 04/01 and 10/07/2026 (Yahoo Finance data), about $36.7 billion. Over a regular 6-hour-30-minute session, or 390 minutes, that averages out to about 127,000 shares per minute. It's in that flow that high-frequency systems fight over fractions of a cent.

For a manual trader, the same math doesn't work out: a one-cent spread and a per-order fee alone already exceed a tenth-of-a-cent gain per share, and the order arrives too late to catch the opportunity anyway.

How HFT Affects the Market

More Liquidity Most of the Time

With systems offering both buy and sell orders all the time, spreads tend to be tighter for every participant, including the ordinary investor.

Liquidity That Disappears Under Stress

In extreme moments, many of these systems pull back to avoid risk, right when liquidity is needed most. The most cited case is the flash crash of May 6, 2010, when the Dow Jones index fell about 9% in minutes and recovered most of the drop the same day. US regulators found that high-frequency traders amplified the selling pressure in that episode.

What Changes in Reading the Chart

Tiny, repeated swings with no news behind them are usually noise from systems trading with each other, not a shift in opinion about the asset. On charts measured in seconds or a few minutes, this noise weighs more. On hourly, daily, or weekly charts, it dilutes, and analysis, context, and risk management matter more than speed again.

Limitations

HFT doesn't explain every fast price move, and not every algorithm is predatory. Many industry figures, like profits and share of volume, are research estimates and vary a lot depending on the source and the market.

Common Mistakes About High-Frequency Trading

  • Trying to manually replicate arbitrage or spread-capture strategies.
  • Thinking a ready-made bot running on a home computer competes with systems in colocation.
  • Interpreting every tiny price wiggle as a signal with its own meaning.
  • Trading on second-by-second charts without accounting for how automated noise dominates that timeframe.
  • Blaming HFT for every loss, instead of reviewing your own plan.
