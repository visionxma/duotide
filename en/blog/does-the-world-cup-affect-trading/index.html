Does the World Cup Affect Trading? What the 2026 Data Shows

Quick answer

The World Cup affects the market mainly through indirect paths: less investor attention during matches, lower volume, and worse execution in the local market. At the 2026 World Cup, the Ibovespa's average volume fell 15.3% compared with the six prior weeks, while the S&P 500's stayed about the same. The tournament isn't a buy or sell signal.

The financial market doesn't trade soccer match results. It trades expectations. Still, every World Cup brings the same question: does it move currencies, stocks, and indices? The paths exist, but they're almost always indirect: investor attention, liquidity, mood, exposure of specific companies, and spending on tourism and advertising.

Since the 2026 World Cup was played in the United States, Canada, and Mexico between June 11 and July 19, we can look at the numbers instead of staying in theory. We compared the Ibovespa and the S&P 500 in the six weeks before, during, and in the six weeks after the tournament, using daily data from Yahoo Finance.

What changes in the market during a World Cup

The most studied effect isn't a goal moving the price, but the public stopping paying attention to the market. Academic research using 2010 World Cup data found a drop in exchange trading activity during national team matches. Fewer people watching the screen tends to mean thinner volume, wider spreads on some assets, and slower reaction to news.

The other paths depend on something concrete happening: hotels, airlines, and payment providers can sell more in host cities, and a sponsor gains visibility. That only becomes a price factor when it shows up in quarterly results or in an analyst's forecast revision.

How we measured the effect: what we compared

We split three session windows: April 29 to June 10 (before), June 11 to July 19 (during), and July 20 to August 31 (after). For each one, we calculated three numbers from the daily bars:

  • Average daily volume reported by the source for the index.
  • Average daily move, in absolute terms: how much the index moved, up or down, from one close to the next.
  • Cumulative move of the index over the window.

Real example: Ibovespa and S&P 500 at the 2026 World Cup

The chart shows the Ibovespa from April to September 2026. The World Cup covers the stretch from mid-June to mid-July, right after a sharp drop.

Ibovespa: lower volume during the tournament

The Ibovespa's average daily volume was 15.3% lower during the World Cup than in the six prior weeks and 8.8% lower than in the six weeks that followed. The average daily move also fell: 0.94% before, 0.72% during, and 0.75% after. That's consistent with the idea of less attention, but it doesn't prove causation, as the next item shows.

Context weighs more than soccer

Before the World Cup, the Ibovespa had been dropping 10.60%: it closed April 28 at 188,619 points and June 10 at 168,619. During the tournament it rose 3.02%, to 173,714 points on July 17. The period's low didn't come during the World Cup, but afterward, on August 14, at 164,835 points. A market that just dropped 10% naturally tends to change pace; separating that from a World Cup effect using daily data isn't possible.

S&P 500: almost no difference in the host country

In the United States, which hosted most of the matches, the S&P 500's average daily volume during the World Cup was practically the same as in the six prior weeks (0.2% higher). The average daily move was 0.61% before, 0.67% during, and 0.55% after, and the index rose 2.62% during the tournament's window. The more visible drop in volume came afterward, in August, a traditionally weaker month in the northern hemisphere.

How to use this information

In short-term trades

The most useful practical effect is operational. If your country's national team plays during the session, expect worse execution in the local market: choppier candles, moves that don't hold, and less friendly spreads. That's a reason to reduce size or avoid that time window, not a buy or sell signal.

In stocks tied to the event

Tourism, aviation, beverages, sporting goods, and media gain attention. Before treating that as a thesis, look for the path to the price: bookings above expectations, a company comment in its earnings, an analyst forecast revision. Without that, the headline is just context.

In the host country's index

The S&P 500 data in 2026 shows that a large, global market rarely changes behavior because of a tournament, even as the host. Rates, inflation, employment, and company earnings remain the main drivers.

Limitations

The numbers above use daily bars, which don't show what happens during each match's exact hours; the attention effect is intraday. The windows also include holidays and news that have nothing to do with soccer. A single World Cup is a small sample: it's useful for illustration, not for building a rule.

Common mistakes when linking the World Cup to the market

  • Creating simple rules like “the champion country's market rises” or “the sponsor's stock rises.”
  • Attributing to the World Cup a move that has a macroeconomic explanation, like rates or company results.
  • Forgetting that local liquidity drops during matches and trading with the same position size.
  • Drawing conclusions from a single tournament, or from daily data, about an effect that happens in a matter of hours.
  • Treating a sports headline as an investment thesis without confirmation in the numbers.

If you want to observe the effect yourself, use a demo account during the next major sporting event: note the match times, compare the 5- or 15-minute chart's behavior during those hours with normal days, and log what you saw. Your own data is worth more than any guess.
