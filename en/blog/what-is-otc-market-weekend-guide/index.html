What the OTC Market Is: A Guide to Trading Outside Business Hours

Duotide Team · 09/24/2026 · 4 min read

Many beginners quickly discover that financial markets don't all run 24 hours a day the same way. The stock exchange has session hours, the commercial currency market closes on weekends, and even within the week there are low-liquidity windows. That's the context in which the OTC market emerges, short for over-the-counter, a form of trading that doesn't depend on a centralized exchange running fixed hours.

Instead of all orders going through a single exchange order book, OTC works through contracts between the parties, brokered by the trading platform, with reference prices formed from averages of quotes from different sources. This lets synthetic assets based on stocks, indices, or currencies keep a quote and be traded even when that asset's "official" market is closed, like on weekends and holidays.

Why OTC exists

Think of someone who only has free time to study charts and trade on Saturday morning. If the currency pair or stock this person follows only traded during business-day hours, this trader would be shut out most of the time. The OTC market was created to fill this gap, offering tradable versions of these assets outside the traditional session, usually marked with an "OTC" tag next to the asset's name on the platform.

Important differences from the regular session

The first difference is liquidity. Outside normal trading hours, volume tends to be lower, which can mean sharper, less predictable moves in the face of any news. The second is price formation: since there's no single centralized market open, the OTC asset's quote is calculated based on formulas and averages defined by the broker, which can create small differences from the "official" price once the traditional market reopens.

The third difference, and perhaps the most relevant for risk management, is the behavior around the regular market's reopening. It's common for an asset traded in OTC mode over the weekend to show a gap relative to the previous close on Monday morning, reflecting news and events that happened while the traditional market was closed.

How to prepare to trade in OTC mode

The first step is treating off-hours trading with the same care as regular hours, not less. Lower liquidity calls for proportionally more conservative positions, since the same move in money can represent a bigger percentage change on an asset with fewer trades closed per minute.

The second step is following the economic calendar even on days when the traditional market is closed. News coming out on Saturday or Sunday — election results, monetary policy decisions in other countries, geopolitical events — can influence the OTC quote even before the main market reopens.

The third point is using risk management tools, like the stop, even more disciplined. If an OTC asset usually has less liquidity, pre-defined loss and gain limits prevent a sudden swing, with no real volume behind it, from knocking the trader out of the trade in an uncontrolled way.

A practical example

Suppose a trader trades a contract based on a R$ 1,000 stock index, on a Saturday, in OTC mode. They decide to risk at most 2% of the account balance on a trade, which on a R$ 5,000 account means R$ 100 of maximum risk. If the asset moves more sharply than on a normal business day, precisely because of lower liquidity, this R$ 100 limit prevents a single weekend trade from eating into a disproportionate share of capital.

Advantages and limits of trading outside business hours

OTC's main advantage is flexibility: those who work during the week get the chance to study and trade on weekends, without depending on a specific exchange's opening hours. That expands market access for those with a tighter routine on business days.

The limit, on the other hand, is precisely the lower liquidity and the different price formation from the traditional exchange, which demands more caution than trading during peak hours. Neither of these characteristics makes OTC better or worse — just different, and that difference needs to guide position size and the kind of expectation you bring to the screen.

To wrap up

The OTC market is an access tool, not a shortcut to guaranteed results. It expands the number of hours during which you can watch and trade an asset, but in exchange it demands more attention to liquidity, position size, and the news that keeps coming out even when the traditional exchange's doors are closed. Trading, inside or outside business hours, always involves risk of loss, and it's worth studying each OTC asset's behavior before treating it as a perfect equivalent of its regular-session version.
