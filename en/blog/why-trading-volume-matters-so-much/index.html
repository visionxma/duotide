Why Trading Volume Is So Important

Duotide Team · 09/24/2026 · 4 min read

When a price rises or falls, the first question every trader should ask isn't just "where did the price go", but "how many people took part in this move". That's exactly what trading volume shows: the number of contracts, shares, or lots traded within a given period. Without this information, a price chart tells only half the story.

In this article, you'll understand why many analysts consider volume just as important as price itself, and how to use this information without overcomplicating your analysis.

What volume actually represents

Volume is the count of trades closed on an asset within a time interval — it can be the volume of a 5-minute candle, an entire day, or a week. It usually appears as a histogram just below the price chart, with taller bars during moments of higher activity and shorter ones during moments of lower activity.

Volume alone doesn't say whether the price will rise or fall. What it measures is the intensity of investor participation in that move — and it's that intensity that helps tell apart a consistent move from a weak one that can easily reverse.

Volume confirms the strength of a move

One of the most common uses of volume is confirming whether a price rise or drop has support. Imagine an asset normally traded with an average of 2 million units a day. If in a single day the price rises 4% with a volume of 6 million units, that indicates strong buying interest backing that rally. But if the same 4% advance happened with only 800,000 units traded, the move would have far fewer people behind it — and less support to continue.

The same logic applies to support and resistance breakouts. A resistance breakout accompanied by above-average volume has a much better chance of continuing than a breakout on weak volume, which is often just a short-lived move before the price falls back inside the previous range.

Divergence between price and volume

Another valuable signal is when price and volume move in directions that don't match. If an asset keeps making new highs, but volume shrinks with each new high, that's read as a sign of weakening: fewer and fewer people are willing to buy at those higher prices, which can precede a correction or reversal.

The opposite is also observed at market bottoms: declines accompanied by shrinking volume can indicate selling pressure is running out, even while the price is still falling.

Volume and liquidity in practice

Besides helping read trends, volume is also a practical liquidity indicator. High-volume assets usually have smaller spreads (the difference between buy and sell price) and faster order execution. Very low-volume assets, on the other hand, can have wide spreads and more difficulty entering or exiting a position at the desired price, especially on larger trades.

Because of that, before trading a lesser-known asset, it's worth checking its average daily volume. Consistently low volume is a warning sign of additional risk, regardless of what the price chart is showing.

How to incorporate volume into your analysis

Volume works best as a confirmation tool, not as an isolated entry signal. A practical sequence is: first identify what the price is doing (trend, breakout, reversal), then check whether volume supports that reading. A price signal with matching volume carries more weight than the same signal on below-average volume.

Platforms like Duotide let you display the volume histogram alongside the price chart, which makes it easier to compare the two visually. It's worth spending time watching, on assets you already know, how volume behaved before important moves in the past — that helps calibrate what counts as "high volume" or "low volume" for that specific asset.

Like any technical analysis tool, volume doesn't guarantee being right. It reduces uncertainty by showing the degree of participation behind a move, but the final decision to trade should always factor in the risk taken and the real possibility of loss.
