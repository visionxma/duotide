Penny Stocks: What They Are, How They Work, and the Risks

Quick answer

Penny stocks are shares traded at very low prices, generally from small companies. In the United States, the SEC uses stocks below $5 as a general reference. They attract attention because of the price per share, but they concentrate three above-average risks: low liquidity, little public information, and greater exposure to price manipulation.

Stocks priced in cents spark a seductive idea: buying thousands of shares with little money and watching the value multiply. There are stories of significant gains with this type of stock, but they also concentrate some of the highest risks in the entire stock market.

This text doesn't recommend buying or selling any specific stock. The goal is to explain how these shares work and show, with numbers, why the cost and risk of trading them are usually higher than they appear.

What a Penny Stock Is

The term comes from the United States, where a penny is the one-cent coin. The SEC, the US market's regulator, uses stocks trading below $5 per share, issued by small companies, as a general reference. Many of them trade outside the main exchanges, in over-the-counter markets, where disclosure requirements are lighter.

In Brazil, the term is used informally for stocks worth a few reals or centavos. B3 requires the quote to stay above R$ 1.00: a stock that stays below that value for an extended period gets notified, and the company usually carries out a reverse split, combining several shares into one to raise the unit price.

In general, these are companies with a small market value, little financial track record, a crisis situation, or operations in speculative sectors. That helps explain both the low price and the high risk.

Why Penny Stocks Draw So Much Interest

  • Accessible price: it's possible to buy thousands of shares with little capital, which gives a false impression of affordability.
  • Eye-catching percentage swings: a stock that goes from R$ 0.50 to R$ 1.00 has doubled in value, even though the difference in reals looks small.
  • Success stories: isolated cases circulate far more than the loss cases, creating the impression that such gains are common.

A Low Price Isn't a Cheap Company

A stock's price, by itself, doesn't say whether it's expensive or cheap. What matters is the market value (price × number of shares) compared with the company's profit, revenue, and debt. A company with 2 billion shares at R$ 0.30 is worth R$ 600 million on the exchange, more than a company with 10 million shares at R$ 30.00, worth R$ 300 million. If the first one posts a loss and the second one a profit, the penny stock can be the more expensive of the two.

Practical Example: What Low Liquidity Costs

The numbers below are a hypothetical example, built to show the math. Imagine a stock with the best bid at R$ 0.18 and the best ask at R$ 0.22. The midpoint price is R$ 0.20, and the difference between the two sides (the spread) is R$ 0.04.

The Cost of Getting In and Out

  • Anyone buying at market pays R$ 0.22. With R$ 1,100.00, they get 5,000 shares.
  • If they need to sell right away, they receive R$ 0.18 per share: 5,000 × 0.18 = R$ 900.00.
  • Immediate loss: R$ 200.00, or 18.2% of the amount invested, without anything having changed at the company.

On a large, liquid stock, the spread is usually one centavo on a share worth tens of reals, less than 0.1%. On the example penny stock, it equals 20% of the midpoint price (0.04 ÷ 0.20). Just to break even, the stock needs to rise from R$ 0.18 to R$ 0.22 on the bid side, a 22.2% gain.

When There's No Buyer

Suppose that, at the time of selling, there are only bids for 2,000 shares at R$ 0.18 and the rest of the order book sits at R$ 0.15. To sell all 5,000 at once, the investor receives 2,000 × 0.18 + 3,000 × 0.15 = R$ 360.00 + R$ 450.00 = R$ 810.00. The loss reaches R$ 290.00, or 26.4% of the R$ 1,100.00, just from the lack of a counterparty.

The Risks That Usually Stay Hidden

Low Liquidity

With few trades a day, an order of moderate size can move the price disproportionately. That applies on the way in and, especially, on the way out, when the investor wants to sell quickly.

Little Reliable Information

Smaller companies, especially those traded over the counter, usually disclose less financial data. That makes any analysis harder and increases reliance on rumors, forums, and promises nobody has checked.

Price Manipulation

Because they have a low price and little volume, penny stocks have historically been the favorite target of pump-and-dump schemes: a group buys cheap, spreads optimistic news to attract buyers, and sells at the top, leaving the price to collapse. Regulators like the SEC and Brazil's CVM regularly publish warnings about this type of fraud.

Dilution and Bankruptcy Risk

Struggling companies frequently issue new shares to raise cash, which dilutes existing shareholders. And some of them simply stop existing, taking the stock's value to zero.

How to Evaluate a Penny Stock More Cautiously

Check the Volume and the Spread Before Any Order

Look at the average daily volume and the order book. If the spread is more than a few percentage points, the cost of trading is already high before any price move.

Limit the Position Size

A common rule is to limit exposure to very speculative assets to a small fraction of your capital. In a R$ 20,000.00 portfolio, a 2% cap means at most R$ 400.00 in this type of stock, an amount whose total loss doesn't jeopardize the rest of the plan.

Use Limit Orders

Market orders on illiquid stocks can be executed at prices much worse than the last trade. A limit order sets the maximum buy price or the minimum sell price.

Look for Official Sources

Financial statements, material disclosures, and filings with the CVM or the SEC are worth more than any social media post.

Limitations

None of these precautions eliminates the risk. They reduce the cost of trading and the size of the possible damage, but a small company can lose nearly all its value in a few days.

Common Mistakes with Penny Stocks

  • Thinking a stock is cheap just because it costs cents, without looking at the company's results and market value.
  • Ignoring the spread and finding out, when it's time to sell, that the trade already started with a double-digit loss.
  • Buying on tips from groups or forums that promise fast, sure gains.
  • Using market orders on stocks with few trades per day.
  • Putting a large slice of capital into a single speculative stock.
  • Counting only the stories of those who won and forgetting the companies that disappeared.

To practice without risking money, use a demo account to simulate entries and exits on low-liquidity assets, noting the spread at the time of the order and the price you'd get on the way out. Logging those costs over a few weeks shows, with your own numbers, how much liquidity weighs on the result.
