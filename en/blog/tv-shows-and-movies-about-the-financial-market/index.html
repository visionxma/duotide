TV Shows and Movies About the Financial Market

Quick answer

Among the best productions about the financial market are the shows Industry, Billions, and Black Monday, the movies The Big Short, Margin Call, and Too Big to Fail, and the documentary Inside Job. They help you understand crises, pressure, and psychology, but they're entertainment: none of them teach method, and the all-or-nothing bets that make for good scenes are the opposite of risk management.

Not every lesson about the financial market has to come from spreadsheets and charts. TV shows, movies, and documentaries that portray the routine of banks, crises, and Wall Street culture help you understand the psychology behind decisions, even when the story is dramatized.

The list below sorts the productions by type: fiction about the market's day-to-day, reenactments of real crises, and programs with real traders. For each one, we point out what's worth paying attention to and what shouldn't be carried over to your own account.

Fiction shows about the market's day-to-day

Industry (HBO and BBC, since 2020)

It follows recent graduates competing for permanent spots at the fictional investment bank Pierpoint, in London. It does a good job showing the pressure of starting a career, the trading desks, and the weight of an execution mistake. It's worth watching how the characters handle losses: almost always with more risk, never with less.

Billions (Showtime, 2016 to 2023)

It pits a hedge fund manager, Bobby Axelrod, against a federal prosecutor. The interest for anyone studying the market lies in the themes of insider information and conflicts of interest, which explain why there are so many rules about who can trade what, and when.

Succession (HBO, 2018 to 2023)

It revolves around the family that controls a media conglomerate. It isn't about trading, but it shows control disputes, acquisitions, and the effect of news on a stock's price, topics that come up in any analysis of a listed company.

Productions about real crises

Black Monday (Showtime, 2019 to 2021)

A dramatic comedy set around the crash of October 19, 1987, when the Dow Jones index fell 22.6% in a single session, the largest daily percentage drop in its history. The tone is exaggerated on purpose; the historical fact is real.

The Big Short (2015)

Adam McKay's film based on Michael Lewis's book. It follows investors who realized, before most people, that US mortgage-backed securities were rotten. It's the best entry point for understanding how a credit bubble grows while almost no one is looking closely at the numbers.

Margin Call (2011)

It shows 24 hours inside an investment bank that discovers its positions are worth far less than the models said, at the start of the 2008 crisis. It's useful for seeing how the risk of a leveraged portfolio can be too big to unwind without a loss.

Too Big to Fail (2011)

An HBO television film based on Andrew Ross Sorkin's book. It recreates what happened behind the scenes in the weeks around Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy filing, on September 15, 2008, and the decisions made by regulators and banks under extreme pressure.

Documentaries and shows with real people

  • Inside Job (2010): Charles Ferguson's documentary on the causes of the 2008 crisis, winner of the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature.
  • The Ascent of Money (2008): historian Niall Ferguson's series on the history of money, credit, insurance, and stock exchanges.
  • Million Dollar Traders (BBC, 2009): fund manager Lex van Dam hands real money to eight beginners and follows what happens. It shows, with no script, how a lack of method weighs more than a lack of talent.
  • Wall Street Warriors: a documentary series about the routine of traders, analysts, and brokers in New York.

Practical example: what an all-or-nothing scene would really cost

Fiction loves the trader who puts everything into a single position and wins. Run the math on the other side with a historical number. A full R$ 10,000 account invested in a portfolio tracking the Dow Jones on October 19, 1987 would have lost 22.6% in a single session: R$ 2,260. To get back to R$ 10,000, the remaining R$ 7,740 would need to rise 29.2%, because recovery is always calculated on a smaller base.

Now the same account with a 1% risk rule per trade and a stop set before entry: the planned maximum loss would be R$ 100. On a panic day like that one, the stop could get filled worse than expected, because the price jumps through levels, but the order of magnitude is still completely different. That difference is what movies rarely show, and it's what keeps someone in the market after a bad day.

How to watch and actually learn

Keep a notebook handy

Whenever a scene shows an impulsive decision, write down what a risk management plan would have called for: what the position size would be, where the stop would sit, what the target was. The exercise turns entertainment into a review of habits.

Separate the fact from the script

Dates, crashes, and bankruptcies cited in the productions about 1987 and 2008 can be checked against any historical source. Dialogue, characters, and million-dollar gains in a single afternoon are script. Check the numbers before repeating them.

Notice what the crises had in common

Almost every crisis story features the same ingredients: high leverage, excessive trust in models, and little liquidity when it's time to get out. Recognizing that pattern helps you read market news with more composure.

Limitations

None of these productions teach technical analysis, risk calculation, or order execution. They provide context, not method. And fiction picks the dramatic cases: for every character who nails the big bet, in real life there are many people who made the same bet and lost.

Common mistakes when learning from TV shows and movies

  • Treating a fast-gain scene as a strategy that can be repeated.
  • Thinking the successful trader is the one who risks the most, when the ones who last in the market tend to be the ones who lose little when they're wrong.
  • Repeating numbers from movies without checking the historical source.
  • Watching without taking any notes and never going back to technical study afterward.
  • Forgetting that historical examples describe what happened, not what will happen.

After watching, it's worth taking the reflection into practice without risking money: open a demo account, set your risk per trade before every entry, and record what happened over a few weeks. Your trading journal will teach you more than any script.
