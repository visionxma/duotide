Trading Journal: Why to Keep One and How to Build Yours

Quick answer

A trading journal is the log of every trade: what you did, why you did it, and the result measured in multiples of the risk (R). It serves to separate luck from method and show the gap between the plan and what you actually executed. It works when it's short: eight fields, under two minutes per trade, and a review on a set date.

Almost every trader starts a trading journal excited and stops filling it in within a few weeks. The reason is rarely a lack of discipline: it's that the journal was born too big, with twenty columns, colors for every emotion, and the promise to log everything. After one busy month, nobody opens the spreadsheet anymore.

A journal only works if it's simple enough to fill in under two minutes per trade. The goal isn't to document the trader's life, but to create a mirror of what they actually do.

What a trading journal is and why to keep one

It's a spreadsheet, notebook, or app where every trade becomes a row with the same fields. Three reasons justify the work:

  • Memory is selective. It's easy to remember the trade that worked and forget the three that failed for the same reason. The log doesn't edit anything.
  • A small sample is misleading. Twenty trades say little about a strategy. Only with 50, 100, or more logged entries can you tell, with any confidence, whether a setup has an edge or the result was coincidence.
  • The plan and the behavior split apart. A good part of a beginner's losses comes from the gap between what they should have done and what they did. Without a log, that gap is invisible; with one, it becomes a number.

The 8 fields that actually matter

  • Entry date and time: helps spot patterns by time of day and day of the week.
  • Asset: the exact pair or stock, not the category.
  • Setup name: the most important field. Use three to six fixed categories, such as breakout, trend pullback, or moving average crossover, and don't invent a new one every week.
  • Direction: long or short. A lot of people are much better at one side than the other.
  • Entry, stop, and target: the three prices. Without the stop you can't calculate the result in R.
  • Size in risk: as a percentage of capital, for example 1%, not as a fixed number of shares or lots.
  • Result in R: how many times the initial risk the trade returned, such as +2R, −1R, or +0.3R.
  • Followed the rule? (yes/no): was the trade within the plan, or was it a rule break?

Leave out the confidence rating, how many screens you looked at, and the dollar amount on its own. These fields don't add clarity and tend to be the first ones to get the journal abandoned.

How to calculate the result in R

R is the distance between the entry and the stop, that is, how much you accepted losing per unit. The result in R is the gain or loss per unit divided by that risk:

Result in R = (exit price − entry price) ÷ (entry price − stop), for long trades.

The advantage is comparing trades on different assets and sizes on the same scale. A US$300 stock and a US$200 one turn into comparable numbers.

Real example: three journal entries with 2026 quotes

The three trades below use real daily quotes and rules defined before the entry. They describe what happened, not what will happen.

Entry 1: Apple, stop triggered with a gap

Buy on May 29, 2026, at US$312.06, stop at twice the 14-day ATR (US$5.57), at US$300.92. Risk: US$11.14 per share. On June 9 the stock opened at US$300.28, below the stop, and the exit filled there. Result: (300.28 − 312.06) ÷ 11.14 = −1.06R. Followed the rule? Yes. The loss came in over 1R because of the gap, and the journal shows that instead of hiding it.

Entry 2: Apple, time-based exit

Buy on July 31 at US$308.91, with the ATR at US$9.87 and a stop at US$289.17 (a risk of US$19.74). The rule was to exit after ten sessions if neither the stop nor the target was hit. On August 14 the stock closed at US$305.93: (305.93 − 308.91) ÷ 19.74 = −0.15R. Followed the rule? Yes.

Entry 3: Nvidia, moving average crossover

Buy on August 6 at US$218.99, on the crossover of the 20-day average above the 50-day one, with a stop at US$205.00 (a risk of US$13.99). On October 7 the trade was still open, with the stock at US$237.47: (237.47 − 218.99) ÷ 13.99 = +1.32R marked to market.

Adding up: −1.06 − 0.15 + 1.32 = +0.11R. With a 1% risk on a R$5,000 account, each R is worth R$50, and the total amounts to R$5.50. Three entries prove nothing about the setups, but they already show two useful things: the gap made a loss bigger than planned, and the time-based exit cut short a trade that went sideways.

How to use the journal

Review on a set date

Weekly or biweekly, on your calendar. Group the trades by setup and calculate, for each one, the win rate and the average result in R. Patterns tend to show up after a few dozen entries.

Read the rule column before anything else

If most of the losses come from trades outside the plan, the problem isn't the strategy, it's the execution. That calls for a behavior adjustment, not a new indicator.

Compare planned R with realized R

Losses systematically bigger than −1R point to gaps, poorly placed stops, or stops moved midway. Gains consistently much smaller than the target point to early exits out of fear.

Limitations

The journal shows the past of your sample, which is small next to the market's history. A good streak can be luck, and changes in the market can take away the edge of a setup that used to work. It improves decisions; it doesn't eliminate risk.

Common mistakes in a trading journal

  • Creating too many fields and abandoning the journal the first busy week.
  • Logging only the money result, without the reason for the entry and the stop.
  • Inventing a new setup name for every trade, which blocks any comparison.
  • Skipping the log for bad trades or ones that broke the rule.
  • Never rereading what was logged.
  • Drawing conclusions from ten or fifteen trades.

To get started, build a spreadsheet with the eight fields and log every trade you make on a demo account over the coming weeks, including the ones that went wrong. At your first review, calculate the average result in R per setup and the percentage of trades that stayed within the rule: these two numbers say more about your trading than any memory does.
