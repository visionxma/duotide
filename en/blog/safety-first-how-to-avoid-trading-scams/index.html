Trading Scams: How to Spot and Avoid the Common Ones

Quick answer

Trading scams almost always combine a promise of high, fixed returns, pressure to decide fast, and your money in someone else's hands. To avoid them, check whether the company has regulator authorization, deposit only into accounts in your own name, test withdrawals with a small amount first, and distrust any guaranteed return: real markets have up months and down months.

Wherever money is moving and people are starting something new, there's also someone trying to take advantage of it. Trading is no exception: the curiosity of those just starting out, combined with the desire for fast results, feeds a parallel market of scams that keeps changing contact channels but has repeated the same script for years.

Recognizing the pattern behind each approach is the most effective defense, because the scammer depends on surprise and urgency. This guide gathers the most common scams, shows with numbers why certain promises don't add up, and explains how to check who's on the other side.

What characterizes a trading scam

Almost every scam combines three elements:

  • A promise of high, predictable returns: fixed percentages per day, week, or month, as if the market offered guaranteed returns.
  • Urgency: limited spots, a bonus that expires today, an opportunity that only exists right now.
  • Someone else controlling your money: you deposit into an account that isn't yours, or hand over passwords and access.

When all three show up together, the odds of it being a scam are very high, no matter how professional the site or the profile looks.

The most common scams

A manager who trades for you

Someone presents themselves as a successful trader and offers to trade your money in exchange for a share of the profit. In the first few days, the dashboard shows gains. When you try to withdraw, fees, taxes, or blocks show up. Managing other people's money requires regulator authorization; without it, it's illegal.

A pyramid scheme dressed up as an investment

The scheme pays returns to the first participants with the money from those who join later. It works as long as new people keep joining and collapses when new sign-ups slow down. The classic tell is the emphasis on referring friends, with bonuses for each referral.

A fake platform

A website mimics a trading platform, shows quotes and a growing balance, but the money is never actually put into any market. The scam shows up at withdrawal time, when they ask for an extra deposit to release the funds.

A signal room with guaranteed results

Groups that sell signals with a 90% win rate and only show screenshots of winning trades. Not every signal group is a scam, but a result with no complete, auditable track record proves nothing.

A miracle robot

Programs sold as automatic and foolproof, sometimes with a monthly fee, sometimes requiring a deposit on a platform the seller recommends. No robot eliminates market risk.

Fake support and money recovery

After someone falls for a scam, profiles show up promising to recover the money for a fee. It's the second scam on the same victim.

Practical example: the math behind the promises

A common promise is 1% a day. It sounds small, but with about 252 trading days in a year, 1.01 raised to 252 gives 12.27: $1,000 would turn into $12,274 in a year, a gain of more than 1,100%. Another common promise, 10% a month, turns $1,000 into $3,138 over 12 months (1.10¹² = 3.14).

Compare that with what real markets did in the 12 months through October 7, 2026, according to daily closing quotes:

  • S&P 500: from 6,714.59 points on October 7, 2025 to 7,801.77 on October 7, 2026, up 16.2%.
  • Bitcoin (BTC/USDT): from $121,332.95 to $83,321.81, down 31.3%.

One of the world's leading stock indexes had a good year and rose 16.2%. Bitcoin, famous for its volatility, fell by almost a third. Neither one delivered the same result every month. Anyone promising a high, fixed return, every month, with no down months, is describing something real markets don't do.

How to protect yourself from trading scams

Check the registration

In Brazil, anyone who offers securities, manages portfolios, or gives investment advice needs authorization from the CVM, the securities regulator. The CVM's website has a lookup for registered participants and a list of alerts about irregular offers. In other countries, look up your local regulator. If the company doesn't show up with any regulator, that's already an answer.

Keep the money in your own name

Only deposit into accounts opened in your own name, at institutions you've verified yourself. Never transfer to an individual's personal account, never hand over passwords, and never install remote-access software at a stranger's request.

Be wary of urgency and secrecy

A real opportunity doesn't vanish in an hour, and it doesn't depend on you not telling anyone. Pressure to decide right now is a sales technique, and in a scam it's the central one.

Test withdrawals early

On any new platform, make a small deposit and a small withdrawal before putting in any larger amount. Trouble withdrawing is the clearest sign of a problem.

Limitations

Being registered with a regulator cuts the risk a lot, but it doesn't protect against market losses: trading remains risky even in the right place. And scammers copy names, logos, and addresses from real companies, so check the official site by typing the address yourself, not by clicking links you receive.

Common mistakes that lead to scams

  • Believing screenshots of gains as proof of results.
  • Handing money to someone else to trade for you without checking their authorization.
  • Paying a fee to be able to withdraw your own money.
  • Accepting contact from strangers on social media and messaging apps as if it were official support.
  • Increasing the amount invested because the dashboard shows gains you haven't withdrawn yet.
  • Paying someone who promises to recover money lost in another scam.

What to do if you fell for a scam

Stop sending money immediately, even if they promise to release the balance after one more payment. Save conversations, receipts, and addresses. Notify your bank as soon as possible, file a police report, and report it to the regulator. Change your passwords and turn on two-factor authentication on your accounts.
