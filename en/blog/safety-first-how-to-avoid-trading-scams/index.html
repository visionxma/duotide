Safety First: How to Avoid Trading Scams

Wherever there is money moving and people getting started with something new, there's also someone trying to take advantage of it. Trading is no exception: the curiosity of beginners, combined with the desire for quick results, has created a parallel market of scams that has repeated itself for years, changing the platform of contact but using basically the same script. Recognizing the pattern behind each approach is the most effective defense, because the scammer relies precisely on surprise and urgency to work.

This article gathers the most common scams involving trading and what to do to avoid falling for them, plus simple habits that greatly reduce the risk of having your account or money compromised.

Scam 1: the fake account manager

Someone reaches out via WhatsApp, Telegram, or social media introducing themselves as an expert, manager, or even a broker employee, offering to trade for you or guide your entries in exchange for sending money to a personal account. No serious broker asks for direct transfers to a third party's account or promises to manage your money outside its own platform. If the offer involves sending money for someone else to manage, it's a scam.

Scam 2: signal groups with guaranteed returns

Closed groups promise to double your capital in a few weeks, showing profit screenshots and testimonials from other members. In practice, these screenshots are easy to fake, and the real business model is usually charging an entry fee for the group or earning a commission for referring new people — the promised return never comes consistently. No trading strategy guarantees fixed profit, because every market involves real risk of loss.

Scam 3: phishing and fake login pages

A link received by email or message leads to a page nearly identical to your broker's, asking for your login and password. Once you type in your details, they go straight into the hands of whoever created the fake page. Before entering your password on any link you receive, check the full address in the browser bar, letter by letter, and be wary of any urgent request to confirm account details. A simple tip is to never click directly on a received link: open a new tab and type the broker's address from memory or from your saved browser favorites.

Scam 4: fake support and requests for verification codes

A contact identifies themselves as technical support and asks for the verification code (OTP) sent by SMS or app, claiming they need to confirm your identity or fix an account issue. This code exists precisely to prevent someone else from accessing your account — no legitimate support needs it to help you. Sharing this code is the same as handing over the keys to your account to whoever is on the other end of the line.

How to protect yourself in practice

  • Always use the official website or the app downloaded directly from the official store, never links received from third parties.
  • Enable two-factor authentication on your account and never share verification codes with anyone, not even with someone claiming to be support.
  • Be wary of any promise of guaranteed, risk-free returns, or a foolproof strategy — this kind of language is the trademark of financial scams.
  • Never transfer money to the personal account of someone who promised to trade, manage, or multiply your capital.
  • Independently verify who is contacting you: call the broker's official channels before acting on any unusual request.

What to do if you've already fallen for a scam

Gather screenshots of the conversations, transfer receipts, and any data about the contact, file a police report, and notify the bank or payment institution used in the transfer as soon as possible — in some cases, it's still possible to contest or reverse the transaction if you report it quickly. It's also worth warning other people in your circle, since scammers often repeat the same approach with several contacts on the same social network, frequently choosing victims within the same group of friends or coworkers.

The security of your account on Duotide or any other platform depends both on the technical protection offered and on your own habits: a strong password, two-factor authentication, and wariness of any unsolicited contact form the first line of defense against practically all the scams described here.
