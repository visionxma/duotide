SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic's Mega-IPOs: What Changes in the Market

Quick answer

Mega-IPOs like SpaceX's, and the possible ones from OpenAI and Anthropic, pull capital and attention toward an entire sector and can force index funds to buy the new shares, selling off a bit of everything else. They don't change the market's rules, but they tend to increase volume and volatility around the theme. The OpenAI and Anthropic offerings may still never happen the way they've been discussed.

SpaceX's stock market debut wasn't just a large transaction: it was treated as the start of a mega-IPO season capable of shifting how the market prices entire sectors tied to space, artificial intelligence, and computing infrastructure. With OpenAI and Anthropic pointed to as the next candidates, the question is whether listings of this size actually change how the market works, or whether they're just another cycle of euphoria around well-known names.

This piece organizes the points discussed about this movement, based on public information, and uses real 2026 prices (Yahoo Finance) to show how the artificial intelligence theme was already moving the market. Nothing here is investment advice.

What a mega-IPO is

An IPO is an initial public offering, the moment a company sells shares on the exchange for the first time. A mega-IPO is an offering of exceptional size, whether in the amount raised or in the company's valuation. What sets these cases apart is the ripple effect: a listing of this scale pulls money, attention, and institutional capital toward a single stock and, by extension, toward the entire sector.

The pattern SpaceX left behind

SpaceX's offering drew attention for setting aside an unusually large share for retail investors, reportedly up to 30% of the total, a rare level for deals of this size. With a valuation approaching $2 trillion, the company entered the market already among the most valuable in the world, with demand from investors in the United States, Europe, the United Kingdom, and Asia.

Why an OpenAI offering would carry its own weight

An OpenAI listing would be the first chance for ordinary investors to buy into the company most associated with the modern AI boom. A public valuation for it would shape how the market sees the entire ecosystem: chipmakers, cloud, software, and energy. CEO Sam Altman himself summed up the needed caution by saying an IPO is just a financing event: the value would depend on the company's ability to sustain the enormous investment advanced AI requires.

Where Anthropic would fit in

Anthropic, the creator of the Claude assistant, built much of its business around enterprise customers, with a reported valuation in the hundreds of billions of dollars. If both went public, the market would have, for the first time, a direct comparison between two competing approaches in the same sector: one more consumer-facing, the other more enterprise-facing.

How a mega-IPO affects indices and funds

Passive funds track indices. When a new company joins an index, those funds are required to buy the stock in proportion to its weight, and to do that they sell off a bit of everything else. A hypothetical example: a $1 billion fund tracking an index where the new company ends up weighing 2% needs to buy $20 million of it and sell $20 million from the rest of the portfolio. Multiply that across every fund tracking the same index and the flow adds up fast.

In practice, each index's rules (minimum listing time, profitability, free-float shares) delay or limit that entry, and the weight is calculated only on the shares actually trading in the market. Because of that, the effect tends to be spread out over time, rather than concentrated on the debut day.

Real example: the AI theme on the Nasdaq in 2026

The chart shows the Nasdaq Composite index from April 1, 2026 to October 7, 2026. Over the period, it went from 21,840.95 to 27,538.69 points, up 26.09%, with a low of 21,371.32 on April 2 and a high of 27,722.75 on October 6. The more diversified S&P 500 rose 18.65% over the same stretch.

The theme doesn't rise evenly

Even within the AI-linked universe itself, the results were very different between April 1 and October 7, 2026: AMD rose 207.25% and Micron 195.77%, while Oracle ended down 1.15% and Vertiv down 4.97%. Nvidia rose 35.12%. Betting on the theme isn't the same as picking the right company.

Deep drops along the way

Even with the index up for the period, the Nasdaq got as much as 9.8% below its closing peak on July 29. Some theme stocks suffered far more: Oracle fell as much as 53.7% from its closing peak through July 24, and Micron 39.1% through July 29. A crop of mega-IPOs concentrated around the same theme tends to amplify this kind of swing, because it pours even more capital and expectation into the same place.

How to follow a mega-IPO season

Separate expectation from fact

News about dates, valuations, and retail allocations move the prices of sector companies before any offering actually happens. Follow what has been officially filed, not what's being speculated.

Watch the effect on companies already listed

Competitors and suppliers tend to react to news of a big IPO. Sometimes they rise on the valuation comparison; sometimes they fall because money rotates into the new listing.

Be careful with the first few days

In the first sessions, an IPO's price tends to swing a lot, with little historical data and a lot of pent-up demand. There's no chart to read and no pattern to compare it to.

Limitations

An OpenAI or Anthropic listing may never happen, or may happen under conditions quite different from what's been discussed. The numbers above describe the 2026 market, not the behavior of stocks that don't exist yet.

Common mistakes around mega-IPOs

  • Buying on the first day just because of the company's fame, without knowing the valuation and the offering's size.
  • Thinking every stock in the theme will rise together, when the dispersion between them was huge in 2026.
  • Trading based on rumors about offering dates and prices.
  • Concentrating the portfolio in a single theme because of the headlines.
  • Reading the numbers in this piece as a forecast: they describe what happened, not what will happen.
