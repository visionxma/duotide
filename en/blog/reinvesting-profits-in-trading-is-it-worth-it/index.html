Is Reinvesting Trading Profits Worth It? How to Do It Right

Quick answer

Reinvesting trading profits is worth it when the strategy has already shown a positive expectancy over a large sample of trades and the risk per trade stays the same percentage of the balance. In the first few dozen trades, the gain from reinvesting is small; what usually blows up accounts is raising the risk percentage after a good streak.

After a streak of profitable trades, a natural question comes up: is it worth reinvesting the profit to trade with larger amounts, or is it better to withdraw part of the money and keep trade size the same? There's no single answer. It depends on how much of the recent result is real consistency and how much is normal short-term variation.

Treating the two as if they were the same is the most common mistake made by those who start reinvesting too soon. In this guide, we run the numbers with concrete examples to show what reinvestment adds, what it doesn't add, and how a single change in behavior can wipe out months of gains.

What it means to reinvest profits in trading

Reinvesting means leaving the profit in the account and calculating the size of the next trades based on the updated balance. If you risk 1% per trade, 1% of a larger account is a larger amount of money. The effect is the same as compound interest: gains start earning on a growing base.

The point many people forget is that the rule works both ways. When the account drops, 1% of the new balance is less money, and the position size automatically shrinks. This protects the account during bad streaks, but it also makes recovery slower.

How to calculate the effect of reinvestment

Consider a $2,000 account, 1% risk per trade, a target of twice the risk (1:2 ratio), and a 50% win rate. Let's compare two ways of trading:

  • Fixed dollar risk: always $20 per trade. Each win earns $40 and each loss costs $20.
  • 1% risk on the current balance (reinvesting): each win multiplies the account by 1.02 and each loss, by 0.99.

Results after 20, 100, and 200 trades

  • 20 trades (10 wins): fixed risk ends at $2,200; reinvesting, $2,204.87. A difference of less than $5.
  • 100 trades (50 wins): fixed risk reaches $3,000; reinvesting, $3,256.85.
  • 200 trades (100 wins): fixed risk reaches $4,000; reinvesting, $5,303.55.

The math is simple: with reinvestment, each pair of one win and one loss multiplies the balance by 1.02 × 0.99 = 1.0098. Over 100 pairs, 2,000 × 1.0098¹⁰⁰ = $5,303.55. The lesson is in the numbers: reinvestment barely changes anything in the short term and only makes a difference over hundreds of trades, as long as the strategy has a positive expectancy. If it doesn't, reinvesting only speeds up the loss.

Practical example: what wipes out the gains

The danger is rarely in reinvestment itself, but in the risk increase that tends to come along with confidence. Look at the same $2,000 account:

  • With 1% risk reinvested, 10 wins in a row take the account to $2,437.99.
  • Feeling confident, the trader raises risk to 5% per trade.
  • Then come 4 losses in a row: 2,437.99 × 0.95⁴ = $1,985.76, below the starting capital.
  • Had they kept 1%, the same 4 losses would leave the account at 2,437.99 × 0.99⁴ = $2,341.92.

Four losses in a row seem rare, but they aren't. With a 50% win rate, the chance of seeing at least one streak of 5 losses in a row within 100 trades is about 81%, and for 6 losses, about 55%. With a 40% win rate (60% losses), a streak of 6 losses in 100 trades shows up in about 87% of cases. These are calculated probabilities, not predictions: they show that long streaks are part of the game.

Another number that weighs against raising risk: an 18.55% loss (the effect of the 4 losses at 5%) requires a 22.8% gain just to get back to the starting point.

How to reinvest profits with discipline

Wait for a minimum sample size

Before reinvesting, have at least 50 to 100 recorded trades with the same strategy. Ten good trades say almost nothing: in the example above, the difference between reinvesting or not over 20 trades was less than $5. There's no rush.

Keep the percentage risk, not the enthusiasm

Reinvesting correctly means keeping the same risk percentage on a larger balance. Raising the percentage is a different decision, and it should only be made based on numbers, never right after a good streak.

Withdraw part of the profit

A balanced alternative is to withdraw a fixed fraction of the month's profit (for example, half) and reinvest the rest. You grow more slowly, but you turn part of the result into money that's out of risk and reduce the temptation to increase position sizes.

Set a loss limit for review

Set a point, such as a 10% drop from the account's peak, where you stop, review your trades, and temporarily reduce risk. This keeps a rough patch from turning into a spiral.

Limitations

All the calculations above assume the same win rate and the same risk/reward ratio over time, which doesn't happen in practice: the market changes and the strategy can get worse. They also don't include costs, such as spread and fees, which reduce the expectancy of each trade. And the bigger the position, the bigger the emotional pressure, something no formula measures.

Common mistakes when reinvesting profits

  • Reinvesting after just a few good weeks, without enough of a sample to separate consistency from luck.
  • Raising the risk percentage along with the balance, instead of keeping the same percentage.
  • Forgetting that reinvestment also shrinks the position during drawdowns, and getting frustrated with the slower recovery.
  • Never withdrawing anything and treating the account balance as something that only exists on screen.
  • Ignoring trading costs in growth projections.
  • Projecting the future from one exceptional month.

To practice without risking money, use a demo account to simulate both approaches side by side: record the result of each trade in dollars and in multiples of risk (R). After a few dozen trades, compare the balance with fixed risk versus reinvestment. Your track record shows whether the strategy has a positive expectancy before making any decision to grow position size.
