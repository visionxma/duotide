STARC Bands: How This Volatility Indicator Works

Duotide Team · 09/24/2026 · 4 min read

Among the volatility indicators less known to the general public, STARC Bands occupy an interesting spot: they combine the simplicity of a moving average with the sensitivity of the ATR (Average True Range) to draw a channel that adapts to the market's real rhythm, rather than to a fixed percentage.

What STARC Bands are

STARC stands for Stoller Average Range Channel, created by Manning Stoller. The indicator starts from a simple moving average, usually of 5 or 6 periods, plotted in the middle of the channel. The upper band is calculated by adding a multiple of the ATR to that average, and the lower band by subtracting the same multiple. Unlike Bollinger Bands, which use the standard deviation of the closing price, STARC Bands use the real range of highs and lows, which makes them particularly sensitive to sharp changes in intraday volatility.

How the indicator behaves

When volatility rises, the ATR increases, and the bands move away from the central average. When volatility falls, the bands come closer together. This creates a dynamic channel: instead of a price breaking through a fixed limit, it actually needs to move more than the recent range to touch the upper or lower band, which helps filter out noise in calmer markets.

How to interpret the signals

The most common reading is mean reversion: when the price touches or goes beyond the upper band, it's interpreted as a move stretched upward relative to its own recent pace, subject to a correction back toward the central average. Touching the lower band suggests the mirrored reasoning. This reading works best in markets oscillating within a range, without a strong defined trend.

In markets with a clear trend, the behavior changes: the price can walk along the upper band for several candles during a strong uptrend, or along the lower band during a strong downtrend. That's why many traders combine STARC Bands with a trend indicator, such as a longer-period moving average, so they don't interpret a band touch as a reversal signal within a trend that's actually just getting started.

An example with numbers

Suppose an asset with a 6-period simple moving average at R$ 50.00 and a 14-period ATR of R$ 1.20. Using a multiplier of 2, as is common in this strategy, the upper band sits at R$ 50.00 + (2 × R$ 1.20) = R$ 52.40, and the lower band at R$ 50.00 − (2 × R$ 1.20) = R$ 47.60. If the price rises to R$ 52.60, slightly exceeding the upper band, a trader following the mean-reversion logic could consider this a sign of exhaustion in the recent rally, looking for a correction back toward R$ 50.00 — always with a defined stop in case the move continues.

Building a simple strategy with STARC Bands

A didactic way to test the indicator is to combine three elements: first, identify whether the market is ranging or trending by looking at a longer-timeframe chart; second, use the touch on the STARC Bands as an attention trigger, not as an automatic order; third, wait for an additional confirmation, such as a reversal candle or a crossover of short moving averages, before considering the entry. This extra layer of confirmation reduces the number of false signals that any band indicator generates on its own.

Risks and precautions

The main risk of any mean-reversion strategy is trading against a strong trend that's just getting started. A price that touches the upper band may not be overstretched — it could be at the start of a consistent move that will keep touching that band for a long time. That's why position size should be set with the possibility that the signal is simply wrong, and the stop should be placed at a point that clearly invalidates the reversal reading.

Wrapping up

STARC Bands offer a practical way to measure how far a move has strayed from its own recent volatility pace, but like any technical indicator, they work best as part of a set of confirmations, not as an isolated signal. Testing the indicator on different assets and timeframes before trading with real money helps you understand under which market conditions it tends to work best, and in which it generates more noise than useful signal.
