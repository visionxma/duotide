Short-Term Stock Trading Guide: 4 Steps

Trading stocks over short time frames — from a few minutes to a few days — calls for different criteria than those used by long-term investors. Liquidity, volatility, and proximity to price-moving events start to matter more than the company's long-term fundamentals. This guide lays out four steps for structuring this kind of trading with more rigor.

Step 1: choose stocks with enough liquidity

A thinly traded stock can have a spread — the difference between the buy and sell price — much wider than a high-volume stock, which already eats into the result before any price move even happens. For short trades, stocks with high average daily volume tend to have more predictable execution, without big price jumps caused simply by a lack of counterparty at the moment of the order.

Step 2: understand the stock's volatility, not just the market's

Two stocks in the same sector can have quite different volatilities. A simple way to measure this is to look at the average daily percentage change over the past few weeks: a stock that usually moves 1% a day requires a different risk management approach than one that usually moves 4% over the same period. Trading a more volatile stock short-term can generate bigger results, but requires a proportionally smaller position size to keep the same risk in money.

Step 3: map out the busiest trading hours

In most stock markets, the open and close of the session concentrate the day's highest trading volume, with sharper moves, while the middle of the session tends to be calmer. For short-term traders, knowing in which of these windows your own strategy works best avoids trading during low-liquidity hours, when the price can react less predictably to any larger order.

Step 4: define the entry criteria and position size before anything else

After choosing the stock and the time, what's left is defining exactly what will trigger the entry — a level breakout, a reaction to a reported earnings result, a moving average crossover — and calculating the position size from the maximum accepted risk, not from how much "seems reasonable" to invest at that moment.

A complete numerical example

Suppose a stock trading at R$ 32.00, with a historical average daily change of 2%, suggesting a typical range of about R$ 0.64 a day. A trader with a R$ 5,000 account, willing to risk 1% per trade (R$ 50), decides to trade a breakout above R$ 32.50, with a stop at R$ 31.90 — a distance of R$ 0.60 per share. The maximum position size, respecting the risk limit, would be R$ 50 ÷ R$ 0.60 ≈ 83 shares, a simple calculation that avoids committing more capital than planned, even if the conviction about the breakout seems strong.

Watching out for trading costs

In short-term trading, the number of trades tends to be higher, which makes each trade's costs — fees and spread — weigh proportionally more on the final result than in a long-term trade. It's worth considering this accumulated cost when assessing whether a short-term strategy really pays off compared to an approach with fewer trades and longer time frames.

Building a watchlist

Instead of trying to trade any stock in the market, a common practice is to keep a short list — five to ten stocks — with already known liquidity and volatility, reviewing that list periodically as each stock's behavior changes over the months. This concentrates the study on assets whose behavior is already familiar, instead of starting the analysis from scratch every day.

Logging every trade to review later

Keeping a small record with date, stock traded, reason for entry, position size, and final result helps you see, after a few weeks, whether the chosen stocks really match the expected liquidity and volatility pattern, or whether the watchlist needs adjustments. This simple habit also reveals whether the most significant losses happened because of execution failure, an unsuitable stock choice, or trading outside the planned hours, which helps fix the process more precisely than just looking at the account's final balance.

Practical conclusion

Trading stocks short-term can generate faster results than a long-term approach, but it also demands more execution discipline, attention to liquidity, and strict control of costs and risk per trade. As with any short-term strategy, the risk of loss is real and immediate, and position size should always reflect that before any entry.
