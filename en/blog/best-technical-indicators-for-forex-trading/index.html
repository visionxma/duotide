Best Technical Indicators for Trading the Forex Market

Anyone starting to study the currency market quickly runs into dozens of technical indicators available on charting platforms. The problem usually isn't a lack of tools, but an excess: stacking similar indicators on the same chart just repeats the same information under different names.

A more efficient way to build an analysis is choosing indicators that answer different questions — one for trend, one for momentum, one for volatility — instead of adding up several that say the same thing. Here are four examples and how they complement each other.

Moving averages: for seeing the trend

The simple moving average (SMA) calculates the average of the closing price over a defined number of periods. A 20-period SMA on a pair like USD/JPY, for example, adds up the last 20 closes and divides the result by 20.

The most common use is comparing two averages of different lengths: when the shorter average crosses above the longer one, the market is usually gaining buying strength; when it crosses below, selling strength. Averages work well for identifying the overall direction, but lag behind the price — by definition, they look backward.

RSI: for measuring momentum

The Relative Strength Index (RSI) ranges from 0 to 100 and measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes. Values above 70 usually indicate an overbought asset, and values below 30, oversold.

That doesn't mean an automatic reversal — a pair in a strong trend can stay overbought for a long time. The RSI is more useful as a second filter: if the moving average already indicates an uptrend and the RSI pulls back from 75 to 55 without the price falling proportionally, that suggests a healthy pause within the trend, not its end.

MACD: trend and momentum in the same indicator

The MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) is the difference between two exponential averages, traditionally 12 and 26 periods, accompanied by a third 9-period average called the signal line. When the MACD line crosses above the signal line, momentum is gaining buying strength; the reverse crossover suggests the opposite.

Since it combines averages of different lengths, the MACD ends up serving both trend and momentum purposes, which makes many people consider it redundant when using simple moving averages at the same time — it's worth choosing one of the two for the same purpose, not both together.

Bollinger Bands: for measuring volatility

Bollinger Bands are made up of a central moving average, usually 20 periods, and two bands plotted two standard deviations away from it. When volatility falls, the bands narrow; when volatility rises, they widen.

Suppose EUR/USD has its upper band at 1.0980 and the lower one at 1.0820, with the central average at 1.0900. The total distance between the bands is 160 pips. If, days later, that distance drops to 60 pips, it's a sign volatility has plunged — historically, compression periods like this tend to precede stronger moves, although they don't indicate that move's direction.

How to combine without overdoing it

A lean combination would be: a moving average to confirm the direction, the RSI to assess whether the move is already stretched, and Bollinger Bands to understand whether volatility favors an entry now or calls for more patience. This covers trend, momentum, and volatility without repeating the same reading three times.

An example of a combined reading

Suppose a 10,000-unit EUR/USD lot (a micro lot), where each pip is worth about US$ 1. The 20-period moving average points upward, the RSI is at 58 — no sign of overbought — and the Bollinger Bands have just narrowed from 150 to 70 pips apart, indicating recently low volatility.

This combination suggests a market in an uptrend, not stretched, and about to come out of a period of accommodation. A trader who decides to trade this reading can set the entry near the moving average, a stop loss 30 pips below it, and a target 60 pips above — a 1-to-2 risk-reward ratio. In this example, a 30-pip stop on a micro lot would represent a potential loss of approximately US$ 30 if the scenario doesn't play out.

It's worth remembering that no technical indicator predicts the future — all are calculations based on past price. They help organize the chart's reading and set objective entry and exit criteria, but trading the forex market involves real risk of loss, even when several indicators point in the same direction. Before applying any combination with real money, it's worth testing it thoroughly on a demo account, logging the results of each signal.
